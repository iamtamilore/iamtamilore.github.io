RL vs Genetic Algorithm for Surge Pricing

Two ways to learn a pricing policy, tested against each other on 637,976 real Boston ride records.

1. The problem

Surge pricing decides what multiplier to charge based on the situation right now. Raining at 6pm with more riders than drivers is not the same as a dry Tuesday afternoon, and the price should not be either.

Most surge rules are written by hand: if it is raining or rush hour, charge 1.5x. That captures something real, but it is a blunt instrument. It treats every wet rush hour the same, and it was never tested against the alternative.

The question here is whether an agent can learn a better policy than that rule, and whether two very different learning methods arrive at the same answer.

2. Results

Average daily revenue, evaluated on 8 seeds of 30 simulated days each, so every strategy faces the identical sequence of market conditions.

Strategy Avg daily revenue Std Uplift vs fixed
Fixed: always 1.0x $704,646 6,797 0.0%
Rule: 1.5x if rain or rush $760,117 9,098 +7.9%
RL: Q-learning $833,103 13,465 +18.2%
GA: evolved policy $855,244 13,442 +21.4%

Both learned methods roughly double the gain the hand-written rule achieves. The standard deviations are small relative to the means, so the ordering is stable across seeds rather than an artefact of one lucky run.

The rule baseline earning +7.9% is itself a useful result: it confirms weather and time of day genuinely carry pricing signal. The learned methods then roughly double that by tuning the multiplier per state instead of applying one surge level to every rainy or busy hour.

3. How the problem was modelled

A finite Markov Decision Process, which is a way of describing a situation as a set of states, a set of choices available in each, and a reward for choosing well.

State (24 of them). Three variables describe each hour:

  • time bucket: morning rush (07-09), day (10-15), evening rush (16-19), night
  • weather: dry or wet
  • demand level: low, mid, high

That is 4 x 2 x 3 = 24 states. Kept deliberately small: 24 states x 5 actions is 120 Q-table entries, few enough to read and inspect by hand. A larger state space would price more precisely and be impossible to audit.

Action (5 of them). The multiplier to charge: 1.00, 1.25, 1.50, 1.75, 2.00.

Reward. Revenue for that hour, which is the multiplier times the base fare times the number of rides, times the fraction of riders who accept at that price.

One detail that mattered: the raw dataset prices already have Uber's own surge baked into them. Dividing it back out gives the clean base fare, so the agent applies its multiplier from scratch rather than on top of one that is already there.

4. The part I had to invent

The dataset cannot tell you how sensitive riders are to price. It only records demand at the surge levels Uber actually charged, so there is no observation of what demand would have been at a different price on the same day. Worse, Uber raised prices precisely when demand was already high, so high surge and high demand appear together in a way that makes cause and effect impossible to separate.

So the acceptance model is assumed, not measured:

acceptance(m, s) = max(0, 1 - K(s) * (m - 1))
K = base * wet_factor * rush_factor

K controls how fast bookings drop as the price rises, and it is state-dependent because not every rider reacts the same way. Someone stuck in the rain during rush hour needs the ride and will absorb a surge. Someone on a quiet dry afternoon has other options.

I bounded it deliberately. Below 0.15 would mean riders pay literally any price, which no market does. Above 1.2 would mean a 1.1x surge drives away almost everyone, which is equally unrealistic. Stacked multiplications can push K to either extreme, so the bounds keep the simulator honest.

This is the weakest part of the project and section 8 says so plainly.

5. Approach one: Q-learning

Q-learning keeps a table with one row per state and one column per multiplier. Each cell holds the agent's current estimate of how much long-run revenue that choice earns in that situation. Every hour of training nudges the relevant cell toward what actually happened.

Settings, each chosen rather than defaulted:

  • Learning rate 0.1. Only a tenth of the gap between estimate and outcome is applied each time, so a single unusual hour cannot swing the table.
  • Discount 0.9. Next hour's expected revenue counts for 90% of this hour's, which suits a 24-hour episode where early decisions shape later conditions.
  • Epsilon 1.0 decaying to 0.05. The agent starts fully random, knowing nothing, and decays by 0.999 per episode. By episode 3,000 it mostly exploits what it has learned, keeping 5% exploration so it never gets permanently stuck on a habit.

6. Approach two: genetic algorithm

The genetic algorithm attacks the same problem from the opposite direction. Rather than updating one state at a time, it evolves a whole policy at once: a length-24 vector assigning one multiplier to every state.

Each generation runs four steps. Tournament selection, where three candidates compete and the fittest becomes a parent. Uniform crossover, where the child inherits each of the 24 positions from either parent with equal probability. Mutation, replacing a position at random with probability 0.1. And elitism, carrying the top two genomes forward unchanged so a good policy is never lost to chance.

Fitness is average daily revenue over ten fixed-seed simulator days.

7. What the two agents learned

This is the result worth pausing on. Both methods independently discovered the same pricing structure: surge in high-demand and rainy conditions, hold the price in quiet dry periods. Neither was told that rule. One built it up cell by cell from thousands of individual hours; the other evolved it as a whole policy under selection pressure. They converged anyway.

The GA edges Q-learning, +21.4% against +18.2%, and the reason is visible in how it learns. It evaluates all 24 states simultaneously as a single unit, so even rarely-visited state combinations get shaped by the fitness signal. Q-learning can only learn about a state it actually lands in, so rare states get fewer updates.

That advantage comes with a trade-off. Q-learning updates online and would adapt to a shifting market; the GA would need re-evolving.

8. Honest limitations

  • The demand model is assumed, not learned. K, the function controlling how fast bookings drop as price rises, is my own construction. Every revenue figure inherits that assumption, and a different K would move the numbers.
  • The state and action spaces are deliberately small. 24 states and 5 price points keep the policy readable and auditable, but limit how precisely it can respond.
  • It is a simulator. Running an untrained agent against real riders is not an option, so the environment is built from historical data rather than live response.
  • Revenue is the only objective. A real deployment would weigh rider retention, driver supply and fairness, and pure revenue maximisation is not obviously the right target.

9. Run it

pip install -r requirements.txt
python3 data.py     # builds clean_rides.csv from the raw data
python3 run.py      # trains both agents, prints the table, saves the four plots

data.py must run first. Global SEED = 42, so results reproduce exactly.

Data: Uber and Lyft Cab Prices (Boston), Kaggle, 2019. 693,071 rows, reduced to 637,976 after dropping metered Taxi-tier rides that carry no quoted price. Duplicate weather readings collapsed to one entry per location-hour, 6,276 rows down to 3,960.
